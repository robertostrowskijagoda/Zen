What it is

Zen connects you only with people you have met: you add a friend with a QR code or an invitation link. Every phone and computer is a node of a mesh network (Yggdrasil), so messages, calls and files travel between your devices and your friends’, encrypted end to end; public nodes run by volunteers only pass them on and cannot read them. There is no company server that stores, reads or sells them.

Zen also tries a new way of deciding shared matters: the pyramid, built from small cells of people who know each other in person, where votes, trust and a common purse flow from the bottom up, with no headquarters that could be bribed or shut down.

The idea behind Zen

Zen did not start as a social network. It started with anger at how we decide our shared matters. Once every few years we put a cross on a ballot, and then for years we watch people far away from us do what they like with that vote: parties, campaign money, feeds that serve us anger because anger gets clicks. Between elections an ordinary person has almost no say.

I wanted to give people a tool to change that. That is how the pyramid came about: small cells of three people who know each other in person, stacked into one tree. Votes, trust and a common purse flow from the bottom up. You know everyone in your cell personally, and the head of a cell can be replaced in an anonymous vote. There is no headquarters to bribe, scare or shut down, only people’s phones, all counting by the same public rules.

Then I ran into a simple truth: nobody installs a purely political app. People install what helps them every day: to talk to friends, share a photo, arrange to meet. Politics can come later, and only if there is something to build it on.

So I built a social network first. The kind I would want myself: no ads, no tracking, no algorithm deciding what you see, and no company in the middle reading your conversations. It has to be worth installing on its own, even if you never open the pyramid.

The same design that protects your conversations also protects shared decisions. A network of people who really know each other, with no server anyone could switch off with one phone call from above, is exactly the ground a democracy that belongs to people can stand on. Friends first, then cells, then the whole pyramid.

I don’t know if it will work. I am doing this alone, after work, with no company and no investors. What I do know is that you cannot change how we decide together with tools that belong to someone else. If you think so too, install Zen, invite the people you know, and let’s see where it takes us.

— Robert Ostrowski-Jagoda, the author of Zen

Isn’t it a virus? How to check

  • Download Zen only from the install page or the GitHub releases (the address must start with github.com/robertostrowskijagoda/), or get it from a friend who has Zen.
  • Before installing, you can upload the file to virustotal.com: dozens of antivirus programs check it at once.
  • Compare the file with the SHA-256 checksums of the release.
  • The code is open: anyone can review what Zen does.
  • Every official Android release is signed with the same key, so a phone will not install a fake over the real Zen. The key fingerprint (SHA-256):

5C:F8:4B:A5:25:AC:80:4C:84:2F:B5:B9:2F:35:C5:49:F6:C0:7A:84:FB:E0:C2:80:49:13:91:91:C1:EE:F5:14

Why not on Google Play or the App Store?

The main reason is the stores’ legal rules and policies, which Zen by design does not meet:

  • Moderation. Stores require the developer to review reports and remove content and accounts (Apple guideline 1.2, Google Play’s user-generated content policy). Zen has no server and nobody in the middle who could.
  • The pyramid’s currency and wallet. Financial and crypto rules call for a company and financial licences.
  • Paid gates. In-app payments for digital services must go through the store’s billing, with a 15–30% commission.
  • Dating. Stores require age verification and a moderation team.
  • Self-updating and background work. Google Play forbids apps that update themselves outside the store, and the App Store heavily restricts background execution.
  • Developer identity and paperwork. Stores publish the developer’s details and require formalities such as encryption export declarations.

The cost: the App Store charges 99 USD a year, Google Play 25 USD once, and the licences and legal work run into thousands. Zen is made by one person, for free.

A store can also remove an app or demand changes at any time, and nobody, not even a store, needs to know you have Zen. So Zen is installed straight from GitHub and tells you itself when a new version is out: on Android and computers it fetches the update and checks its signature first; on iPhone SideStore installs it.

Support Zen

Zen is free and ad-free and is kept going only by voluntary donations. It is made by one person, with no company and no investors.
